1. HDD Scheduling

A hard disk drive has a moving read/write head. When several requests arrive, the OS may reorder them to reduce head movement and improve service efficiency.

FCFSServe requests in arrival order. Simple and fair, but may require extra movement.
SSTFServe the nearest pending request. Often reduces movement, but distant requests may wait longer.
SCANMove in one direction serving requests, then reverse direction — like an elevator.

Key idea: Scheduling matters most when physical head movement is expensive. SSDs do not have moving heads.

2. NVM Scheduling

Nonvolatile memory (NVM), such as flash-based SSDs, keeps data without power. Unlike HDDs, SSDs do not need a mechanical head to seek a track. Their performance depends on factors such as request queues, controller behavior, parallelism, and flash management.

HDDRotating platters and moving head; seek distance can matter.
SSD / NVMNo moving head; queue management and device parallelism matter.

3. Error Detection and Correction

Storage data may be corrupted by hardware faults or other errors. Error detection notices a problem; error correction can recover the original information when sufficient redundant information is available.

Checksums and CRCs help detect corruption; error-correcting codes (ECC) can correct certain errors. The exact protection depends on the device and code used.

5. Swap-Space Management

When physical memory is under pressure, the OS may move selected memory pages to a swap area on storage, freeing RAM for other work. Accessing swapped-out data is usually slower than accessing RAM.

6. Storage Attachment

Storage can connect directly to a computer or be accessed over a network. Common examples include internal SATA/NVMe devices, USB external drives, and network-based storage.

InternalSSD/HDD connected inside the computer, such as SATA or NVMe.

7. RAID Structure

RAID combines multiple drives into an array to improve performance, availability, or both, depending on the RAID level. RAID is not a replacement for backups.

RAID 0 — StripingSplits data across drives. Can improve performance but provides no redundancy.
RAID 1 — MirroringKeeps copies of data on separate drives. Can survive some drive failures.
RAID 5 — ParityDistributes data and parity across at least three drives. Can tolerate one drive failure.
